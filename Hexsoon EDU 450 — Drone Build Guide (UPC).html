00 · Overview

What we built, and how to read this guide

Starting from a box of parts, we assembled a four-motor drone with an automatic pilot and prepared it for its first flights at the UPC DroneLab.

The drone is a quadcopter built around an Orange Cube autopilot running ArduPilot. It can be flown by hand with the radio transmitter, but it can also be commanded from a ground station — a laptop running Mission Planner — which can additionally fly fully automatic missions defined as a sequence of waypoints. Because the autopilot speaks a documented protocol, teams can even write their own ground station (for example in Python), which is where most of the experimentation, learning and fun begins.

The whole process is organised as a sequence of activities. Each activity below contains the theory you need, precise instructions, the figures referenced in the original build guide, and the short videos that show each operation being performed. Follow them in order: every activity assumes the previous one is finished.

Read

Each step is written in plain English. Parameter names and values appear as MONOSPACE so they are easy to spot in Mission Planner.

Watch

Blue cards open the matching video on YouTube in a new tab. They are the original course videos that demonstrate each operation.

Heed

02 · Inventory

Unboxing

Check that every component and tool is present before assembly begins — it saves hours later.

The kit contains everything needed to assemble and configure the drone. Lay it all out and confirm each item against the parts figure before you start. The original guide also documents where to buy the complete kit and, if you prefer, where to source each component separately.

Before you start

Verify the four motors, four arms, two orange (front) and two black (rear) motor mounts, the power distribution board, four ESCs, propellers (two with a white dot, two without), the Orange Cube kit, GPS, radio transmitter & receiver, telemetry radios, buzzer and the LiPo battery. Missing a part now means a stalled build later.

03 · Mechanics

Frame assembly — part 1

Motors, arms, power distribution board and ESCs. The hands-on activity — best done with good music playing.

  1. Prepare the four motors

    Screw each motor to its mount. Two mounts are orange (they go at the front) and two are black (the rear). Two motors have a white dot on the shaft and two do not — place one white-dot motor on an orange mount and the other white-dot motor on a black mount. Orient each motor so the wires exit in the direction indicated by the small circular slot in the mount.

  2. Prepare the four arms

    Assemble each of the four arms as shown. Figure 3.2

  3. Wire the LEDs to the power distribution board

    Connect each of the four coloured LED wires (one per arm), the POWER2 cable that will feed the autopilot (it comes in the Orange Cube kit), and the double connector for LED control. Protect the last two cables with black insulating tape so they do not chafe on the edges of the black plate the board bolts to. Figure 3.3

  4. Bolt the power distribution board to the top plate

    The two black plates in the Hexsoon kit are different — the upper one is the one shown in the figure. Respect its exact position and orientation. Both the POWER2 cable and the LED double-connector must exit on the top side of the plate. Figure 3.4

  5. Insert one ESC into each arm

    Connect each ESC to the power leads coming from the distribution board, then connect the other end to its motor according to the required direction of rotation. Motors with a white dot turn counter-clockwise; motors without turn clockwise. Figures 3.5 & 3.6

  6. Place arms and motors correctly relative to the top plate

    Simple but critical. Each propeller shape determines the direction the motor must spin so that it pushes air downwards: white-dot propellers must turn counter-clockwise (CCW), the others clockwise (CW). Front motors (orange mounts) are positions 1 and 3; the white-dot/CCW motors occupy positions 1 and 2. Extensions were added to the front motors' braided cables to make later connections easier.

    Do not trust the printed arrows

    Each motor has little arrows printed above the word HEXSOON indicating spin direction (newer kits omit them). These arrows are sometimes wrong. Decide direction from the propeller shape and the position diagram (Figure 3.7) — not from the printed arrows.

  7. Fix the motor to the arm

    Not a trivial operation — it takes some manual dexterity. Figure 3.8

  8. Fix the arms to the top and bottom plates

    Make sure the following cables emerge on the top of the frame: the four braided motor-control cables (two with extensions), the POWER2 cable for the autopilot, the LED double-connector cable, and the cable to power an on-board computer (a Raspberry Pi) in the future. Figure 3.9

  9. Keep cables away from the distribution board

    Route the cables so that any heating of the board cannot affect them. Figure 3.10

04 · Radio

Radio control

Configure the transmitter that will command the motors and the servo, and verify every channel works.

Basic concepts

The transmitter can send signals over up to 24 channels, multiplexed together. Each channel is driven by one actuator on the transmitter — a stick, a switch (SA, SB…) or a knob (S1, S2). The receiver separates the multiplexed signal back into individual channels and outputs each as a PWM signal (up to 8 channels on our receiver). The width of each PWM pulse sets how fast a motor spins or what position a servo takes — this is the basis of all radio control.

How radio control & PWM workYouTube · concepts

  1. Charge the transmitter battery

    Connect the transmitter to a computer with the supplied USB cable. The indicator light turns green while charging and switches off when fully charged. Figure 4.4

  2. Insert the MicroSD card

    The card is not strictly required, but it greatly expands storage for sounds and music that improve the user experience. Figure 4.5

    MicroSD & soundsYouTube

  3. Calibrate the transmitter

    Before configuring it for the drone, calibrate the sticks and switches.

    Transmitter calibrationYouTube

  4. Create the model

    Build a model that maps each actuator to a channel. You can use the OpenTX Companion software (free, from open-tx.org) or create the model directly on the transmitter. First put the radio into USB mode, then create the model.

    Put the radio in USB modeYouTube Create the model in OpenTXYouTube Create the model on the transmitterYouTube
    Check the stick mode

    The video does not show this, but verify the stick mode: the US stick configuration differs from the European one. Confirm it before continuing (Figure 4.6).

    Assign the stick axes to channels as follows:

    OperationStick movementChannel
    Thr — ThrottleLeft stick, vertical — climb / descendCH3
    Ail — RollRight stick, horizontal — move right / leftCH1
    Ele — PitchRight stick, vertical — move forward / backCH2
    Rud — YawLeft stick, horizontal — rotate on its axisCH4

    Also assign switch SB (3 positions) to CH5, switch SF (2 positions) to CH7, and — though the video omits it — switch SC (3 positions) to CH6. Simulate the model to verify it, then save it to the transmitter.

  5. Bind transmitter to receiver

    The binding procedure depends on the receiver protocol — ACCESS or ACCST.

    Bind — ACCESS protocolYouTube Bind — ACCST protocolYouTube

  6. Test the channels

    Connect each motor and the servo to different channels and confirm everything responds correctly.

    Testing motors & servo per channelYouTube
    If a motor spins the wrong way

    During the test you may find a motor turning the wrong direction (the video shows exactly this). Fix it by swapping any two of the three ESC-to-motor wires. It is a tedious fix — you must unscrew the arm and disconnect the LED to reach the connections — but it has to be right before flight.

  7. Invert the Pitch channel

    By default, pushing the right stick forward makes the drone go backwards. If that feels wrong, invert the pitch channel directly on the transmitter. Figure 4.7

05 · Brains

The autopilot

First configuration and calibration of the Pixhawk Orange Cube — the device that turns stick movements into coordinated flight.

Basic concepts

The autopilot generates the right combination of PWM signals to take off, land, move and rotate. It receives your commands through the radio receiver: the receiver's SBUS OUT connects to the autopilot's RCIN input. From now on a stick no longer drives one motor directly — it asks the autopilot to perform a higher-level manoeuvre, and the autopilot works out what every motor should do to keep the drone stable.

To hold stability it fuses data from internal sensors (barometer, accelerometer, gyroscope) and, when available, an external GPS. The flight mode decides which it relies on:

ChannelActuatorMovement
CH1Ail · Rollleft / right
CH2Ele · Pitchforward / back
CH3Thr · Throttleclimb / descend
CH4Rud · Yawrotate on its axis
AltHold

Holds altitude using internal sensors.

Loiter

Holds altitude and XY position using GPS. Needs GPS coverage.

RTL

Return To Launch — flies back to the take-off point. Needs GPS.

We select among three modes with the 3-position SB switch. The autopilot can also fly a full mission (a sequence of waypoints) sent from a ground station — Mission Planner — which also records telemetry for later analysis.

    Select AltHold, Loiter and RTL, and set the RTL altitude (a good choice at the DroneLab is 7 m) via the full parameter list (Figure 5.4).

06 · Integration

Frame assembly — part 2

Mount the autopilot, connect the motors, and add the GPS, telemetry radio and buzzer. After this, the airframe is complete (propellers go on only at flight time).

Helpful walkthrough for this activityYouTube · reference

  1. Fix the autopilot to the top plate

    Centre it precisely

    It is crucial that the autopilot is perfectly centred: the geometric centre of the orange cube must coincide with the geometric centre of the plate. An off-centre IMU degrades flight stability. (See the video at ~4:30.)

  2. Connect the receiver & power the autopilot

    The SBUS OUT → RCIN cable both carries commands and powers the receiver. Mind the polarity of the three pins. Power the autopilot itself through the POWER2 cable from the distribution board. Figure 6.4

  3. Connect the motors to the autopilot

    Motors are numbered as in Figure 6.5 (left); connect each to its matching MAIN OUT output (right). The front motors' cable extensions can help, though a direct connection keeps the wiring tidier. Figure 6.5

  4. Install the GPS receiver

    Mount it at the front of the top plate. Route the black cable inside the support. Connect the red/black connector to CAN1; also connecting the white/green connector to CAN2 is recommended. Figure 6.6

  5. Install the telemetry radio

    One transceiver plugs into the laptop's USB; the other connects to the autopilot's Telem1 input. This link lets Mission Planner talk to the drone in flight, which the USB cable cannot. Figure 6.7

  6. Install the buzzer

    The buzzer connects to the autopilot's USB input. Figure 6.8

  7. Add Velcro & straps for the battery

    Fit the hook-and-loop strips and straps that will hold the battery, with extra Velcro as backup. Figure 6.9

  8. Secure everything

    Fasten the receiver and telemetry radio with Velcro and zip ties so nothing shifts in flight. Figure 6.10

Note 1 — FailSafe

FailSafe tells the autopilot what to do if the system fails. For now we set the radio-loss case. FS PWM is the throttle value below which the autopilot assumes the radio is lost — it must be below the minimum throttle PWM you noted during radio calibration (e.g. 975, which is below 982). A good action on radio loss is Return To Launch (RTL).

Note 2 — Loading recommended parameters

If Mission Planner cannot find the recommended parameter file for the Hexsoon EDU 450, download Hexsoon-edu450.param from our repository and load it with the green Load from file button (top-left). Also, for early flights, reduce the manual climb/descent speed: set PILOT_SPEED_UP from the default 250 cm/s to around 100 cm/s (Figure 7.1).

Watch the battery

Check the charge often (Figure 7.2). Each cell should read at least 3.7 V — if not, it is time to recharge before doing anything else.

08 · Preparation

Simulator, geofences & mission planning

Rehearse everything safely before going to the DroneLab — then fly inside safe limits and let the drone execute missions automatically.

The SITL (Software In The Loop) simulator runs inside Mission Planner and imitates the autopilot's behaviour. It is ideal for training and for testing flight plans in advance, saving time and avoiding costly mistakes on the real aircraft.

The SITL simulator — basicsYouTube

Geofences & missions

A geofence is a boundary the autopilot will not let the drone cross — in Mission Planner you draw a polygon (Figure 8.1) and choose what happens if the drone reaches it. A mission is a sequence of geolocated waypoints the autopilot flies automatically: you define it in Mission Planner, upload it to the autopilot and run it.

09 · DroneLab

Flight tests

With the platform assembled, configured and rehearsed in the simulator, it is time to fly at the UPC DroneLab (Baix Llobregat Campus, Castelldefels).

Fit the propellers only now — white-dot propellers on the white-dot (CCW) motors, the others on the CW motors. Run the pre-flight checklist, confirm GPS lock before any Loiter/RTL work, and keep the first hovers low and short in AltHold.

10 · Data

Log analysis

Every flight writes a dataflash log. Reading it back is how you turn a "that felt wobbly" into an actual fix.

Download the .bin logs through Mission Planner and review the key channels: attitude (desired vs actual roll/pitch/yaw), vibration levels (VIBE), battery voltage/current under load, GPS HDOP and satellite count, and any ERR events. Comparing commanded against achieved attitude quickly reveals tuning or mechanical issues (for example, excessive vibration pointing back to motor balance or a loose autopilot mount).

! · Lessons

Difficulties & solutions

The traps that cost us (and other teams) the most time — and exactly how we got past them.

Printed motor arrows were wrong

The spin arrows printed on some motors contradicted the correct direction. Fix: ignore the print — derive direction from propeller shape and the position diagram (Fig 3.7).

A motor spun the wrong way

Found during the channel test. Fix: swap any two of the three ESC-to-motor wires — which meant unscrewing the arm and disconnecting the LED.

Pitch felt inverted

Right stick forward → drone backwards. Fix: invert the pitch channel on the transmitter.

Odd autopilot behaviour after flashing

Leftover parameters from previous users. Fix: reset all parameters to default right after flashing firmware, then re-apply ours.

US vs EU stick mode

Default stick mapping differed from what we expected. Fix: verify and set the stick mode before building the model (Fig 4.6).

Param file "not found"

Mission Planner couldn't locate the recommended params. Fix: load Hexsoon-edu450.param from our repo with Load from file.

Peripherals not ready at boot

Occasional init glitches. Fix: set BRD_BOOT_DELAY = 3000 to delay startup 3 s so peripherals initialise first.

Telemetry link slower than USB

Expected behaviour, not a fault. Approach: use USB for heavy config, telemetry radio for in-field checks and flight.
